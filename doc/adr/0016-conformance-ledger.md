# ADR 0016 — The specification is checked by a program, and what is unread is counted

Status: accepted, 2026-07-28.

## Context

This project cites ISO 32000-2 constantly and, until the ninth session, **nothing checked a
single citation**. The first tooling ever pointed at them found, over the whole tree rather
than a sample:

| | |
|---|---|
| `§` citations in Rust source | 146 |
| distinct clause numbers cited | 36 |
| **cited numbers that do not exist in ISO 32000-2** | **2** |
| **quotations sampled that are paraphrases inside quotation marks** | **3 of 5** |

`§8.9.6.5` was cited for the colour-key mask array in four source files, an ADR and two
reports; there is no §8.9.6.5. `§11.4.5.6` was cited for knockout groups, which are §11.4.6.
Neither had changed a pixel, and that is the point: a citation is how a reviewer checks the
code against the clause instead of against itself, and this project's best habit — *a
subclause is a checklist* — is worth exactly as much as the number attached to it.

The second problem is larger and has no observable symptom at all. Eight sessions were
steered by two gates, and both take the pdf.js corpus as their universe. They are good gates.
But the ordering they produce is a **demand curve** — features ranked by how many of 974 files
want each — and a demand curve cannot rank a requirement no file exercises, cannot notice a
clause nobody implemented, and declares success the moment the last file goes green. The only
answers to "how much of PDF do we support" were a corpus count, which measures demand, and a
prose self-assessment, which had been **wrong twice**: it called clause 9's encoding
algorithms complete while §9.6.5.4 was one line, and it said Type 3 fonts were reported for
two sessions in which they were not.

## The decision

Two things, one small and one that will take years to fill.

### A checker, in the tree, with no dependency

`tools/conformance` builds the standard's clause index from the 860 numbered headings of
`doc/md/ISO_32000-2_sponsored_EC3.md` — committed, so unlike the pdf.js submodule it needs no
skip path — and fails the build on:

- a `§` citation naming a clause the standard does not have;
- a rustdoc blockquote whose words are not in the clause it is attributed to;
- a blockquote with no citation before it, which is a quotation nothing can check;
- a ledger row that does not match the standard, or claims evidence that is not on disk.

**Quotation marks mean verbatim** is now enforced rather than intended, and the form that
carries the enforcement is the blockquote: this tree quotes plenty of things that are not the
standard — an error message, a warning another renderer printed, a sentence from the ICC
specification, a rhetorical aside — so the checkable ones have to be marked. Paraphrase stays
welcome, and often clearer, in prose without quotation marks.

Comparison normalises layout, Markdown markup and typography, and allows `…` to elide with
the fragments required in order. All three are noise between a PDF converted to Markdown and
a doc comment wrapped at 96 columns; none of them can make a paraphrase pass.

### A ledger, one row per subclause, whose statuses are set by people

`doc/conformance/ledger.toml` holds 823 rows, one per numbered subclause of clauses 7 to 14,
generated by `cargo run -p conformance --bin ledger` and never edited as a *set*. A row's
status is a claim somebody makes after reading that clause against this code, and the checker
verifies only that the claim is **well formed** — the clause exists, the code and test it
names are on disk, an exclusion is one `CLAUDE.md` principle 5 allows. Whether the code
implements the clause is not checkable and is not attempted. That is why the ledger holds
words rather than a computed percentage.

Eight statuses, and they exist to keep five situations from wearing one word — the project
*choosing* (`out-of-scope`), *not knowing* (`unreviewed`), *owing out loud* (`reported`,
`partial`), *owing in silence* (`silent`), and the requirement having no meaning for a screen
(`inapplicable`, `writer-side`).

**`silent` was added while filling the first rows, and it is the status worth hunting.** Every
missing *subsystem* in this tree reports — `LZWDecode`, encryption, Type 3 fonts — because
whoever decided not to build it wrote the report in the same afternoon. The gaps that ship are
the ones *inside* something implemented: `Tr` was parsed with four of its eight modes changing
a clip nobody built, `/SMask` was honoured while `/Mask` beside it was not, knockout groups
composite as though they were not knockouts. Reading the clause is the only thing that finds
those, and `silent` is where the finding goes. The ninth session's first pass produced two:
§11.4.6 and §8.11.4.4.

**`out-of-scope` is the status that would rot first**, so it is the one the reader constrains:
a row may carry it only with an `exclusion` naming one of principle 5's closed list, which is
an enum in the checker. Widening the list means editing principle 5 and the enum together, in
a commit that says so. Without that, `out-of-scope` becomes the graveyard every clause goes to
once it turns out to be difficult, which is precisely the escape hatch principle 5 refuses.

## What was decided against, and why

**A TOML crate.** The ledger's format is four constructs — a comment, a blank line, an
array-of-tables header, and a key holding a string or a list of strings — and
`toml_subset.rs` reads exactly those in under two hundred lines with the grammar stated in
one place. The cost is written down rather than hidden: *this is not a TOML parser*. It
accepts a subset and **rejects** the rest, by line, with a message naming what it expected, so
valid TOML outside the subset fails to read rather than being misread. That property is what
makes a restricted reader safe to build; without it the answer would be a real parser.

The reason to avoid the dependency is not purity. `toml_edit` is in this tree's lock file
already — reached only through `winit`'s Android path, so it does not currently build on
Linux — and adding it would be one line. It is that the conformance gate is the last thing
that should stop running because a dependency did not.

**A computed percentage.** Tempting, and it would be a number that goes up. It would also be
a number nobody could act on, because "82% of clause 12" says nothing about which of the 166
subclauses decide whether a page looks right. The per-clause `unreviewed` count the gate
prints is the honest form: it says where the questions have not been asked.

**Making the first run green.** The rule that a clause the code cites may not be `unreviewed`
found 36 clauses owing a review. Filling 36 rows in one sitting would have produced exactly
the rubber stamp this file argues against — a clause-family review costs 20 to 60 minutes and
produces findings, which is not something to do 36 of against the clock. So `REVIEW_OWED` in
the gate names them, and it is a ratchet in both directions: a clause not on it fails
immediately, and a clause on it that has been reviewed must be deleted from it.

**Scanning the checker's own crate.** It is excluded, and `NOT_SCANNED` says why in full: its
comments name `§8.9.6.5` and `§11.4.5.6` on purpose, because they are what it was built for.
The cost is that its own citations are checked by reading, as everything was before it existed.

## Consequences

The first run of the finished checker was green on 214 citations, which is the *second* run:
the four wrong clause numbers were fixed in the same session, and the checker was then
confirmed to fail when one is put back. Five quotations are verified verbatim, three of which
were paraphrases wearing quotation marks and are now either exact or unquoted.

Reading a clause family produced findings immediately, which is the argument for paying the
20 to 60 minutes:

- **The `/Mask` citations were still wrong after being fixed.** §8.9.6.2 is *stencil* masking
  — an image painting the current colour through its own `/ImageMask` — and the `/Mask`
  entry naming another image is *explicit* masking, §8.9.6.3. Three sites said §8.9.6.2. The
  first correction of the session had been to a number; this one was to an understanding.
- **Stencil masking had no test.** It is implemented, it works, and nothing pinned it. The
  corpus and the oracle cover it in the sense that a page would look wrong — which is not the
  same as a rule that fails by name. `tests/image_masks.rs` now holds it, and was confirmed to
  fail when the sample sense is inverted.
- **§8.9.6.2's last sentence is not implemented**: interpolation should smooth a
  low-resolution stencil's edges, and `/Interpolate` is read nowhere in this tree. Nothing
  else in eight sessions had noticed.

The ledger starts at 717 of 823 subclauses `unreviewed`, and that number may only fall. It is
the project's honest count of unasked questions.
